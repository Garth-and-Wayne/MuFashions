MuFashions — You Already Ate (Self-Released, 2000)
Posted by daveATLsouth  |  March 14, 2004  |  Filed under: reviews, atlanta, indie rock, regional
You Already Ate
Artist:MuFashions
Year:2000
Label:Self-Released
Origin:Atlanta, GA
Tracks:7
★★★★☆

I'll be honest with you: I almost didn't write this one up. You Already Ate has been sitting on my desk since sometime in 2001, when a guy at a MuFashions show at Smith's Olde Bar handed it to me after their set and said "just listen to track two." I took it home, put it on, forgot about it for three years, and found it again last month under a copy of the Creative Loafing from the week the Braves choked in the playoffs. So here we are.

MuFashions were — are? nobody seems to know — a five-piece out of Atlanta by way of West Virginia, which is a sentence I never expected to type. The lineup was Tyler Wolfe on lead vocals and guitar, his brother Nick Wolfe on guitar and vocals, Scott Heuke rounding out what became an unusual three-guitar configuration, Travis Hagner producing, and Chris Cahill handling management with the particular focused calm of a man who has never once doubted that success is right around the corner. They put out this record in 2000, played shows around the Southeast for a couple of years, and then quietly stopped. No announcement. No farewell tour. They were just there and then they weren't, the way a lot of good Atlanta bands end up.

"Three guitars should not work this well. Heuke's contribution sounds inevitable in a way that takes real talent to pull off — the kind of thing you only notice when it isn't there."

The record itself is seven tracks and about 35 minutes, recorded in what sounds like one room by people who understood that the fastest way to kill a good song is to overthink the production. Hagner — who produced the whole thing and is listed in the liner notes only as "T. Hagner, Ponce de Leon sessions" — made the right call on every decision, which is mostly: leave it alone. You can hear the room. You can occasionally hear someone breathing. On the last track, "Goodbye, Decatur," you can hear what sounds like a door closing in the background during the final chord. It stays. Of course it stays.

The songs themselves are something of a puzzle. They're funny without being jokey, specific without being insular, and emotionally messy in ways that feel true rather than manufactured. "Sandwich," the second track and the closest thing to a single this album has, is about exactly what the title suggests — and also, somehow, about something else entirely. Wolfe's vocal delivery is so straight-faced that the whole thing operates in a kind of dual register: you're laughing and you're not, simultaneously, for the entire four minutes. It's a harder trick to pull off than it sounds, and most bands that try it fail. MuFashions don't fail.

Standout Tracks
  • Sandwich — the one you'll want to play for people. Give it two listens.
  • West Virginia — six minutes that earns every one of them.
  • Little Five Points, 11 PM — if you've been there you know exactly what this song is about.
  • Goodbye, Decatur — the closer. Don't skip it.

The three-guitar lineup, which by all rights should be a disaster, turns out to be the record's secret weapon. Heuke's contribution is hard to describe precisely because it sounds inevitable — you only notice what he's doing when you try to imagine the song without it. Nick Wolfe plays melodic where Tyler plays rhythmic and the two approaches create a center of gravity that keeps even the longer tracks from drifting. "West Virginia," which clocks in at over six minutes, should feel indulgent and doesn't. That's not an accident.

I don't know what happened to MuFashions. Their website has been down for at least a year. Cahill, who I briefly spoke to at a Variety Playhouse show in 2002, said they were "still figuring some things out," which is the kind of answer that in retrospect sounds like an ending. Wolfe apparently moved. Heuke I see around sometimes. Nobody seems to know about a second record, though I've heard rumors of sessions.

What I do know is that You Already Ate is one of the better records to come out of Atlanta in the last five years and almost nobody has heard it. There were reportedly 400 copies pressed. I have no idea how many are left. If you find one, don't let it go.
